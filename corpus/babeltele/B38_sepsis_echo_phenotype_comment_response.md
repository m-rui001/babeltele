We read with great interest the study by Shvilkina et al. [ 1 ] recently published in the Journal of Intensive Care .

First, cluster 4, characterized by elevated cardiac index (6.6 L/min/m 2 ) and high left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of mean 75%, did not demonstrate significantly higher in-hospital mortality compared with the normal phenotype.

A cluster of only 41 patients is markedly underpowered to detect a mortality difference of the magnitude reported in larger cohorts, and the absence of statistical significance should not be conflated with evidence of absence of harm. Sato et al. [ 2 ] reported a meta-analysis of 3427 sepsis patients, which demonstrated that an LVEF of ≥ 70% was associated with significantly higher short-term mortality compared with normal LV systolic function (OR 2.37; 95% CI 1.47–3.80). Similarly, Dugar et al. [ 3 ] conducted a retrospective cohort of 3151 sepsis and septic shock patients, identified a U -shaped relationship between LVEF and in-hospital mortality, wherein both severely reduced LVEF (&lt; 25%; OR 2.30; 95% CI 1.52–3.49) and hyperdynamic LVEF (≥ 70%; OR 1.42; 95% CI 1.07–1.87) were independently associated with increased mortality. From a pathophysiological standpoint, hyperdynamic LV function in sepsis is frequently a manifestation of severe vasodilation, catecholamine-driven sympathetic overstimulation, and a high-output shock state.

Second, the omission of standard right ventricular systolic function parameters such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), fractional area change (FAC), and RV systolic velocity (RV S’) from the clustering model, despite their routine availability in echocardiographic assessment, warrants careful consideration.

As such, cluster 3 may more accurately represent a pattern of RV dilation with increased pulmonary afterload rather than true RV systolic dysfunction, a distinction with important implications for both the internal homogeneity of this cluster and the interpretability of its observed highest mortality rate (36.9%).

Third, beyond the authors’ brief acknowledgment of non-standardized echocardiographic timing at 14 days, we wish to highlight a more specific concern with broader implications for the interpretation of all phenotypic clusters.

Cardiac dysfunction in sepsis is a highly dynamic process, influenced by volume status, fluid resuscitation, and vasoactive therapy, all of which may substantially alter echocardiographic parameters within hours. Response to commentary Dear editor, We thank Trieu and Nguyen for their thoughtful discussion and interest in our work. We agree that important questions remain in the definition and clinical implications of echocardiographic phenotypes in sepsis. The authors highlight ongoing challenges in sepsis phenotyping, including variable selection and capturing the dynamic nature of cardiovascular function over time. The absence of a statistically significant increase in mortality in our cohort with elevated left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and elevated cardiac index (CI) compared to the normal phenotype may have been due to the relatively small size of this cluster. However, latent profile-derived phenotypes capture multidimensional patterns incorporating multiple parameters and are not directly equivalent to classifications based solely on one parameter such as LVEF. Given LVEF is highly load-dependent, it may be elevated in various physiologic states including vasoplegia, hypovolemia, right ventricular dysfunction, or driven by increased catecholamine stimulation.

Our cohort was characterized not solely by elevated LVEF but in combination with elevated CI, normal mean RV diameter, and normal mean E/e′ ratio.

Similarly, Geri et al. identified a hyperkinetic cluster characterized by elevated LVEF and CI that was not associated with increased mortality [ 1 ]. While existing literature certainly supports higher mortality in populations defined by an elevated LVEF [ 2 ], there may be heterogeneity within this population. With respect to RV function, we agree that incorporation of systolic parameters such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), fractional area change (FAC) or tissue Doppler S’ velocity would have enriched the analysis. Unfortunately, our institution did not routinely measure these variables during the time period of this study and these were therefore largely unavailable. We intentionally avoided labeling cluster 3 as a phenotype specific to right ventricular systolic dysfunction and rather one of RV dilation and elevated tricuspid regurgitation gradient.

A challenge in interpreting cardiac dysfunction in sepsis is its dynamic nature, and phenotypes may evolve throughout the clinical course.

Further investigation incorporating prospective serial echocardiographic assessments and longitudinal data, as well as integration with biomarkers, will be essential to delineate whether these phenotypes represent transient dynamic states, stable endotypes, or a combination of both.

We agree that continued investigation is necessary to further validate and refine echocardiographic phenotypes in sepsis and to better understand the biological mechanisms underpinning this heterogeneous disease. Ultimately, the integration of prospective serial echocardiographic, clinical, and molecular data may help us advance our understanding of cardiovascular heterogeneity in sepsis, moving the field from descriptive phenotyping toward individualized risk stratification and tailored therapy.
